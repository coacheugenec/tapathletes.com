A

Pitch Types

14 terms
Changeup#
A slower pitch thrown with fastball arm speed to disrupt timing; usually 10–15 mph slower with arm-side fade.
Circle Change#
A changeup gripped with a circle made by the thumb/index finger, adding a slight arm-side fade.
Curveball#
A slower breaking ball with topspin that dives sharply downward.
Eephus#
An extremely slow, high arching “trick” pitch meant to surprise hitters.
Fastball#
A pitcher’s hardest pitch with minimal break.
  • ·Four-seam: Straight, firm, best velocity.
  • ·Two-seam / Sinker: Slightly slower with arm-side run and sink.
  • ·Cutter: Slight glove-side movement to induce weak contact.
Forkball#
A slow splitter-like pitch with the ball jammed between the fingers, producing a big drop.
Knuckleball#
A very slow pitch thrown with almost no spin, causing erratic fluttering movement.
Knuckle-Curve#
A curveball thrown with a bent (knuckled) finger; breaks down like a standard curve.
Palmball#
A changeup held deep in the palm to reduce speed with minimal effort.
Screwball#
A rare breaking pitch that moves toward the pitcher’s arm side (opposite a slider).
Slider#
A fast-breaking pitch with sharp glove-side movement; tighter and faster than a curve.
Slurve#
A hybrid of slider and curve with sweeping diagonal break.
Splitter#
An off-speed pitch with fingers split wide; drops sharply late due to tumbling spin.
Sweeper#
A slider variant with large horizontal sweep and lots of sidespin.
B

Pitch Characteristics

15 terms
Arm-Side vs. Glove-Side Movement#
Arm-side = moves toward pitcher’s throwing arm. Glove-side = moves the opposite direction.
Arm Slot#
The angle of the throwing arm (overhand, ¾, sidearm, submarine). Affects movement and deception.
Command#
Ability to precisely locate pitches where intended.
Control#
Ability to consistently throw strikes and avoid walks.
Effective Velocity#
How fast a pitch feels based on location and extension (e.g., high/inside pitches “play faster”).
Extension#
How far in front of the mound the ball is released; more extension = less reaction time.
Horizontal Break#
Side-to-side movement (arm-side run or glove-side cut).
Late Life#
Informal term for fastballs that stay lively or jump late due to spin and/or extension.
Movement (Break)#
Any deviation from a straight path caused by spin—vertical (rise/sink) or horizontal (run/cut).
Release Point#
Where the ball leaves the hand in height and lateral position; consistency aids deception and accuracy.
Spin Axis#
The orientation of the ball’s spin, determining break direction (e.g., 12–6 curve).
Spin Efficiency (Active Spin)#
How much of the spin produces movement (vs. wasted gyro spin).
Spin Rate#
How fast the ball spins (RPM); higher spin increases movement potential.
Velocity#
Pitch speed (mph); higher velocity reduces reaction time.
Vertical Break#
Upward or downward movement relative to gravity. High backspin = “ride.” Topspin = more drop/sink.
C

Mechanics

17 terms
Arm Action#
The path the throwing arm takes from hand break to release. Smooth, efficient arm action improves timing and reduces injury risk; stiff or late actions can cause mechanical issues.
Arm Slot#
The arm’s release angle (overhand, ¾, sidearm, submarine). It affects the release point and pitch movement.
Balance Point#
The moment at the top of the leg lift when the pitcher is balanced on the back leg. Modern mechanics stress flowing through this phase rather than pausing.
Follow-Through#
The motion after release where the arm decelerates, and the body finishes balanced. Proper follow-through reduces stress on the arm.
Glove-Side Mechanics (Front Side)#
How the lead arm and shoulder behave during the delivery. A firm, stable glove side keeps the front shoulder closed and helps create rotation power; flying open hurts velocity and command.
Hip-Shoulder Separation#
The delay between hip rotation and shoulder rotation. More separation creates powerful “torsion” and generally increases velocity.
Inverted W#
A mechanical position where elbows rise above the shoulders with forearms pointed down. Considered a red flag by some due to possible timing issues and added arm stress.
Leg Kick (Leg Lift)#
The lift of the front leg during the delivery. A higher kick can generate momentum but must remain balanced; pitchers may shorten it from the stretch.
Pronation#
Inward rotation of the forearm after release. Important for changeups and for safely decelerating the arm.
Release Point#
Where the ball leaves the hand in height and lateral position. Consistency improves deception and control.
Scapular Loading (Scap Load)#
Drawing the shoulder blades together during stride to help store energy and generate velocity.
Stretch (Set Position)#
Compact pitching stance used with runners on base. Requires a full stop before delivery; pitchers may use a slide step for quicker timing.
Stride#
The step toward home plate after the leg lift. A strong, directed stride helps generate lower-body power.
Stride Length and Direction#
How far and where the lead foot lands. Longer strides aid extension and velocity; landing too open or too closed affects hip rotation and command.
Supination#
Outward forearm rotation used for breaking balls. Must be done safely because excessive supination can stress the elbow.
“Tall and Fall” vs. “Drop and Drive”#
Two delivery styles:
  • ·Tall and fall — stay upright and glide down the mound.
  • ·Drop and drive — bend the back leg and push forcefully toward the plate.
Most pitchers blend the two.
Windup#
A full pitching motion used with no runners on base, often involving a step back and higher leg lift to build momentum.
D

Data & Metrics

15 terms
Active Spin (Spin Efficiency)#
The percentage of spin that creates movement. Higher active spin = more break.
BABIP Against#
Batting average on balls in play allowed by a pitcher (excluding HRs). Mostly influenced by defense, contact quality, and luck.
CSW% (Called + Swinging Strikes)#
(Called Strikes + Swinging Strikes) ÷ Total Pitches. Measures total strike-getting ability; ~30% is good.
ERA+ / ERA-#
Park- and league-adjusted ERA. ERA+: 100 = average; higher is better. ERA-: 100 = average; lower is better.
ERA#
Earned runs allowed per nine innings. Lower = better.
FIP#
ERA-like metric based only on Ks, BBs, HBPs, and HRs. Removes defense/luck to show pitcher-controlled performance.
K/BB#
Strikeouts ÷ Walks. Higher numbers show dominance and good command.
Quality Start#
6+ innings and ≤3 earned runs. Indicates the pitcher kept the team in the game.
Release Extension#
How far in front of the rubber the ball is released. More extension = higher perceived velocity.
Spin Rate#
Pitch spin in RPM. Higher spin generally increases pitch movement or “ride.”
Strikeout Rate (K% / K/9)#
K%: % of batters struck out. K/9: Strikeouts per nine innings. Higher rates = more swing-and-miss ability.
Vertical & Horizontal Movement#
Measured break (in inches) compared to a no-spin pitch. Quantifies rise/sink and side movement.
Walk Rate (BB% / BB/9)#
BB%: % of batters walked. BB/9: Walks per nine innings. Lower rates = better control.
WHIP#
(Walks + Hits) ÷ Innings. Measures baserunners allowed; lower is better.
WAR (Pitcher WAR)#
Overall value in wins above a replacement pitcher, based on run prevention and workload.
E

Pitch Science & Technology

15 terms
Biomechanics#
The scientific study of a pitcher’s motion using tools like motion capture to measure joint angles, forces, and rotational speeds. Teams use biomechanical reports to identify mechanical strengths or inefficiencies and reduce injury risk.
Edgertronic Camera#
Is a high-speed camera that captures thousands of frames per second to analyze release, spin, and mechanics in extreme detail. It allows pitchers to see exactly how the ball comes off their fingers for precise pitch-design adjustments.
Force Plate Mound#
Measures the ground reaction forces a pitcher generates with each leg during the delivery. It helps identify how well a pitcher uses the lower body and whether force is being directed efficiently toward home plate.
Hawk-Eye#
Is MLB’s optical camera-based tracking system that records pitch movement, player motion, and ball trajectories with high precision. Its enhanced accuracy enables advanced metrics like release extension, detailed spin data, and bat-tracking.
Kinematic Sequence#
Describes the order in which body segments reach peak speed—typically hips, then torso, then arm, then wrist. A proper sequence maximizes energy transfer and velocity while reducing unnecessary stress.
Magnus Effect#
The Magnus effect explains how spin makes a baseball move by creating pressure differences in the surrounding air. Backspin creates rise, topspin creates drop, and sidespin produces horizontal break.
Pitch Design#
Is a data-driven process where pitchers use tools like Rapsodo, TrackMan, and Edgertronic cameras to refine grip, release, and movement. The goal is to craft pitches that pair well together and maximize deception and effectiveness.
Pitch Logic#
A smart baseball with built-in sensors that measure metrics such as velocity, spin rate, and spin axis. It provides instant feedback during bullpen sessions without the need for external tracking systems.
Pocket Radar#
A portable, handheld radar gun that measures pitch velocity accurately. It’s widely used in practice, showcases, and youth baseball because of its convenience and affordability.
Rapsodo#
A portable pitch-tracking device that measures velocity, spin, movement, and strike-zone location using radar and cameras. It provides immediate data for pitch design and mechanical adjustments.
Seam-Shifted Wake (SSW)#
Is an aerodynamic effect where seam orientation alters airflow and creates movement not explained by spin alone. It often adds extra run, sink, or ride to pitches like two-seamers, changeups, and certain fastballs.
Skeleton Motion Detection#
Uses AI to map a digital skeleton onto a pitcher’s body to track joint angles and movement patterns. It allows detailed mechanical analysis without markers or wearable sensors.
Statcast#
Is MLB’s advanced tracking system that measures pitch data, batted-ball data, and player movement using high-speed cameras and radar. It has transformed baseball analytics by quantifying velocity, spin, movement, and athletic performance.
TrackMan#
A Doppler radar system that tracks baseball flight, measuring velocity, spin, movement, and batted-ball data. It was the backbone of Statcast from 2015–2019 and remains widely used in development settings.
Wearables (Pitching Sensors)#
Pitching wearables are sensor-based devices (like Motus sleeves or smart balls) that measure arm stress, workload, and movement patterns. They help coaches monitor fatigue, prevent injury, and guide individualized training plans.
F

Training & Drills

12 terms
Bullpen Session#
A bullpen session is a practice outing on the bullpen mound where a pitcher throws a planned set of pitches between games. It’s a controlled environment used to maintain arm strength and work on mechanics, command, or pitch development.
Flat-Ground Throwing#
Flat-ground throwing is pitching practice done on level ground, usually at about 60 feet, to reduce stress compared to mound work. It’s commonly used for light throwing days, injury rehab, and refining commands or mechanics.
Live BP#
Is a practice session where a pitcher throws to hitters who treat the reps like batting practice rather than structured game at-bats. It gives pitchers live-hitter feedback and gives hitters real pitching without full game pressure.
Long Toss#
Is a throwing drill where players gradually increase throwing distance, sometimes well beyond regulation. It builds arm strength and endurance and usually ends with harder, shorter-distance throws called pulldowns.
Plyo Ball Drills (Weighted Balls)#
Use weighted rubber balls to train arm speed, stability, and mechanical patterns. Common drills like reverse throws and pivot pickoffs strengthen both the accelerating and decelerating muscles of the arm.
Pitchers’ Fielding Practice (PFP)#
PFP trains pitchers to field bunts, comebackers, and cover bases through repeated situational drills. Good PFP improves defensive performance and helps prevent runs during games.
Recovery and Arm Care#
Routines include band work, light weights, stretching, and other methods to protect the arm after throwing. The goal is to maintain arm health, reduce fatigue, and prevent injury throughout the season.
“Running Gun” Throws (Pulldowns)#
Running gun throws involve taking a running start and throwing at maximum intent, often into a net or to a partner. This drill helps build velocity and confidence by allowing the pitcher to move athletically and exceed mound speed.
Simulated Game#
Lets a pitcher face hitter in a controlled setting that mimics real game conditions without being an official game. Coaches can tailor situations, making it ideal for rehab or extra in-game style reps.
Throwing Program (Rehab Throwing)#
Is a structured progression of distances, intensities, and volumes used to rebuild arm strength after rest or injury. It gradually transitions a pitcher from light catch to long toss, bullpens, and eventually game-speed throwing.
Towel Drill#
Replaces the baseball with a small towel and has the pitcher snap it toward a target during a full delivery. It reinforces proper extension, timing, and follow-through by making flaws in mechanics immediately noticeable.
Weighted Ball Program#
Is a planned routine using balls heavier and lighter than a standard baseball to build velocity and arm fitness. These programs can increase arm speed but require careful monitoring to manage added stress on the arm.
G

Pitch Design Terminology

29 terms

Advanced concepts, grouped into nine areas. Several terms here also appear earlier in the dictionary — the definitions below are the pitch-design reading of them.

Pitch Pairing and Tunneling

Pitch Pairing#
Is the deliberate sequencing of two pitches to maximize their combined effectiveness. By varying speed, location, or movement between the consecutive pitches, a pitcher can disrupt a hitter’s timing and expectations.
Pitch Tunneling#
Involves designing two pitches to follow nearly identical trajectories for most of their flight. This makes it difficult for hitters to distinguish between pitches until very late, increasing deception and inducing poor contact or swings and misses.
Spin Mirroring#
Is a pitch pairing concept where two pitches share the same spin axis but rotate in opposite directions. This makes the seam patterns appear like the hitter, making spin-based recognition very difficult, especially when combined with tunneling.
Tunnel Point#
Is the location along a pitch flight where two consecutive pitches begin to separate. The later this point occurs, the more effective the tunnel, giving the hitter less time to detect differences between pitches.

Seam Orientation and Movement Effects

Axis Deviation#
Refers to the difference between a pitch observed spin axis and the spin axis inferred from its movement. A large deviation indicates the seams influenced the ball’s flight, causing it to move in ways not explained by Magnus’ effect alone.
Laminar Express#
Is a one-seam sinker engineered by Trevor Bauer to exploit seam-shifted wake. By orienting the ball so one side is smooth, and the other catches air; it generates unexpected sideways movement, demonstrating how seam placement can enhance pitch movement beyond traditional metrics.
Seam Orientation#
Is the alignment of a baseball’s seams relative to its spin axis. Different orientations affect airflow around the ball, creating movement that deviates from what spin alone would produce, and modern pitch design uses this to optimize grip and maximize seam-shifted wake effects.
Seam-Shifted Wake (SSW)#
Is an aerodynamic effect where a baseball’s seams create an uneven air “wake,” producing lateral or vertical forces beyond the Magnus effect. This phenomenon explains why certain pitches, like two-seam fastballs or changeups, move more or differently than spin alone would predict.

Spin Axis and Tilt (Spin Direction)

Spin Axis#
Is the imaginary line a baseball rotates around during flight, determining the direction of Magnus force and pitch movement. A vertical spin axis creates backspin (like a four-seam fastball with carry), while a horizontal axis produces little Magnus movement, and modern tracking systems measure it in 3D or as 2D “tilt” for coaching purposes.
Tilt (Clock Face)#
Tilt expresses a pitch’s spin axis orientation using a clock-face analogy from the catcher’s view. For example, 12:00 is pure backspin, 6:00 topspin, 3:00 glove-side, and 9:00 arm-side. Adjusting tilt via grip or wrist angle changes movement and is a key tool in pitch design and deception.

Spin Efficiency and Gyro Component

Gyro Degree#
Quantifies the amount of gyro spin on a pitch, ranging from 0° (pure transverse spin) to 90° (pure bullet/gyro spin). Pitchers can adjust wrist position to change gyro degree, with lower values increasing Magnus-effect movement and higher values producing more bullet-like spin, useful for sliders or cutters.
Gyro Spin (Bullet Spin)#
Occurs when the spin axis points directly toward the target, like a bullet or football, producing little to no Magnus-effect movement. Many modern sliders, cutters, and splitters use gyro spin for late action, and high gyro can interact with seam-shifted wake effects to create unique movement.
Spin Efficiency (Active Spin)#
Is the percentage of total spin that contributes to pitch movement. A 100% efficient pitch converts all spin into movement, while lower efficiency indicates wasted gyro spin; multiplying spin rate by spin efficiency gives true spin, the effective spin causing the pitch movement.

Movement Metrics and Concepts

Horizontal Break (Run/Cut)#
Measures the lateral movement of a pitch relative to a spinless trajectory, caused by sidespin. Positive values usually indicate arm-side movement (“run” or “tail”) and negative values indicate glove-side movement (“cut” or sweep), with gyro-heavy pitches showing near zero horizontal break.
Vertical Approach Angle (VAA)#
Is the angle at which a pitch crosses the home plate, usually negative because pitches drop. A steep VAA means the pitch descends sharply, while a flat VAA gives the perception of a “rising” pitch, helping pitchers manipulate hitters’ swing paths based on release height and spin.
Vertical Break (Ride)#
Quantifies how much a pitch rises or falls relative to a spinless pitch due to spin-induced lift or drop. Positive vertical break (ride) means the pitch drops less than expected, while negative vertical break indicates extra downward movement, with backspin fastballs showing strong ride and topspin curveballs showing steep drops.

Release and Deception Factors

Extension#
Is the distance in front of the pitching rubber where the ball is released, usually measured in feet. Greater extension effectively shortens the ball’s path to home plate, increasing perceived velocity and reducing hitter reaction time.
Perceived Velocity (PV)#
Adjusts the pitch’s actual speed based on the pitcher’s extension, showing how fast the pitch “looks” to the hitter. Longer extension increases PV, making even moderate-speed pitches appear quicker, while shorter extension reduces PV.
Release Window#
Is the three-dimensional area from which a pitcher delivers the ball, including height, lateral position, and extension. Maintaining a consistent release window across pitches maximizes deception and supports effective pitch tunneling.

Supination vs. Pronation (Release Action)

Pronation#
Is the inward rotation of the forearm at release, like turning a doorknob left for a right-hander. It is used to reduce spin, add arm-side run or fade, and enhance drop on pitches such as changeups, sinkers, and two-seamers.
Supination#
Is the outward rotation of the forearm at release, like turning a doorknob right for a right-hander. It generates sidespin or topspin for breaking balls like sliders and curveballs, controlling lateral or downward movement.

Modern Pitch Variations and Grips

Gyro Slider#
A slider that relies on bullet (gyro) spin rather than pure sidespin, resulting in a tight, short break with minimal horizontal and upward movement. Often thrown harder than sweeping sliders, gyro sliders drop slightly due to gravity but tunnel well off a four-seam fastball.
One-Seam Fastball#
A fastball (sinker) gripped so only one seam prominently interacts with the air, enhancing seam-shifted wake effects. This grip produces extra arm-side movement compared to a standard two-seamer but can be harder to command.
Splitter (Split-Change)#
A pitch with fingers split wide, producing very low backspin and spin efficiency, causing sharp downward tumble. Modern “kill-spin” splitters maximize drop while minimizing Magnus effect, often appearing like a fastball out of the hand before diving near the plate.
Sweeper#
A slider variant with a maximal horizontal break and minimal vertical drop, designed to sweep dramatically glove-side. Achieved through high spin efficiency and a side/topspin release, sweepers are slower than traditional sliders but excel at generating chase swings out of the zone.

Technology and Tools in Pitch Design

Edgertronic Camera#
Ultra high-speed camera capable of thousands of frames per second. Used in labs and bullpens to visualize the moment the ball leaves the hand, seam orientation, and spin patterns. Ideal for diagnosing grip issues and release mechanics, often paired with TrackMan or Rapsodo data for full pitch design analysis.
Hawk-Eye#
MLB’s current pitch-tracking system (replacing TrackMan in 2020). Use multiple high-resolution cameras to capture the full flight and 3D spin axis of every pitch. Provides direct, in-game measurement of spin axis and movement, enabling advanced analysis like seam-shifted wake effects.
Rapsodo#
Portable camera-based tracking device used in bullpens and labs. Measures velocity, spin rate, spin efficiency, tilt (axis), horizontal/vertical break, and strike location in real time. Newer models can also visualize seam orientation, making it ideal for iterative pitch design and immediate feedback.
TrackMan#
Radar-based pitch tracking system implemented in MLB parks (2015–2019) and widely used in training facilities. Tracks trajectory, spin rate, inferred spin axis, release angle, extension, and movement. While revolutionary for quantifying “stuff” and spin efficiency, pre-2020 TrackMan could not directly measure the spin axis, limiting some advanced analyses.
